# Reading digest — 2026-10-23

Found 10 paper(s) in the last 48h window.

1. **SCOPE: Compiling Natural-Language Policies into Neurosymbolic Predicate Programs**
   Authors: Arnon Mazza, Elad Levi
   Summary: Rather than running NL policies by prompting a large LLM, the authors propose a compiler that turns a written policy into a program made of neural predicates and symbolic combinators, which a small model can then evaluate deterministically. The resulting programs are inspectable, their verdicts are stable across runs, and small models executing them match or beat much larger prompted judges on policy-grounded QA. The contribution is a bridge between informal NL specifications and verifiable rule execution — exactly the kind of spec-to-executable pipeline needed to anchor agent-correctness checks.
   Theme: (3) Spec-driven development and formal verification
   Relevance: Compiles human-written specs into executable, auditable programs — a direct template for turning agent-output specifications into deterministic checkers.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31062

2. **VerifierBench: Benchmarking the Generation of Executable Verifiers for LLM Agents**
   Authors: Jianing Yin, Ziyi Chen, Hao Wang
   Summary: The paper argues that an LLM agent's answers are only as reliable as the verifiers checking them, and defines a benchmark where models must author executable verifiers that both accept all valid solutions and reject invalid ones. Across the collected tasks, generated verifiers frequently over-reject correct solutions because they bake in hidden assumptions the problem never stated. The analysis isolates specific failure modes (missing edge cases, implicit input constraints, test-oracle under-specification) that make generated verifiers brittle in agentic coding loops.
   Theme: (3) Spec-driven development and formal verification
   Relevance: Puts the "write a checker for the agent's output" step itself under a microscope — core to any spec-driven coding-agent workflow.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28679

3. **MolLean: A Process-Verifiable Language for Chemical Reasoning**
   Authors: Jiatong Li, Yatao Bian
   Summary: The authors build a Lean-based domain language in which each reasoning step over a molecule must type-check against an explicit chemical state, so wrong moves are rejected before the next step rather than caught after a final answer. A tool interface returns stepwise feedback to the model, letting agents repair their own derivations instead of accumulating silent errors. Although the domain is chemistry, the mechanics transfer: proof-assistant-backed step verification is one of the cleanest known ways to constrain agent outputs to a formal spec.
   Theme: (3) Spec-driven development and formal verification
   Relevance: Shows how a proof-assistant backend can enforce stepwise correctness on an agent's reasoning trace — the pattern we'd want for formally-checked coding agents.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20459

4. **ToM2Vec: Continuous Theory-of-Mind Tokens for Multi-Agent Coordination**
   Authors: Nithin Sivakumaran, Ali Payani, Hyunji Lee
   Summary: Instead of hand-coding belief models or passing natural-language explanations, this work learns compact continuous "theory-of-mind" tokens that each agent appends to its communication, letting peers condition on inferred intents and action priors. The tokens are trained end-to-end on coordination objectives and generalize across collaborative, competitive, and mixed-motive settings. Reported gains are strongest where partner behavior shifts quickly and standard broadcast communication fails to keep up.
   Theme: (2) Multi-agent frameworks and orchestration
   Relevance: A concrete, trainable communication protocol — a building block for agent-architecture research beyond text-only message passing.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29934

5. **Group-Relative Credit Assignment for Evolving Multi-Agent Teams (GRSE)**
   Authors: Yanan Ma, Yang Wang, David Le
   Summary: In teams where each agent carries its own evolving skill library, the authors assign credit to specific (agent, skill) pairs by comparing many executions sharing the same library configurations rather than scoring whole-team outcomes. The method stays robust when some agents' libraries are deliberately corrupted, and it outperforms baselines on multi-hop QA where skill ownership is ambiguous. The result is a practical scheme for deciding which agent's skill to actually update after a joint run.
   Theme: (2) Multi-agent frameworks and orchestration
   Relevance: Credit assignment is the chronic weak point of agent-team training; this is a targeted mechanism for role- and skill-level attribution.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27227

6. **Hidden Messages Between Diffusion Language Model Agents: Causal Evidence for Latent Communication**
   Authors: Alan-Barsag Gazzaev, Alexey Gavrilov, Sergey Muravyov
   Summary: Using diffusion language models as agents, the authors show that short continuous hidden states passed between them causally change the receiver's answers, not just correlate with them. They also find that when, during the denoising schedule, the hidden state is handed over matters more than how many exchanges happen. The paper argues this latent channel is a real, measurable communication protocol with implications for both coordination design and alignment auditing.
   Theme: (2) Multi-agent frameworks and orchestration
   Relevance: Rigorous causal (not just observational) evidence of latent inter-agent communication — relevant to anyone designing or policing agent protocols.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28905

7. **Building AI Research Teams for Autonomous Discovery of Linear Attention Architectures**
   Authors: Yujian Liu, Li An, Zelin He, Jacob Helwig, Jiabao Ji, et al.
   Summary: The system assembles a persistent team of role-specialized AI researchers (theorist, implementer, experimentalist, critic) that pursue linear-attention variants over multi-day sessions, with an adaptive scheduler reallocating compute toward branches showing promise. Beyond a single project, the paper reports what structural choices (role boundaries, shared memory, review cadence) make the team productive versus thrashing. The write-up includes concrete architectures discovered autonomously, grounding the orchestration claims in real artifacts.
   Theme: (2) Multi-agent frameworks and orchestration
   Relevance: One of the clearer recent case studies of long-horizon, role-partitioned agent orchestration producing real research outputs.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28887

8. **TraceForge: Distilling with Your Own Thinking**
   Authors: Yan Wang
   Summary: Rather than copying teacher chains of thought verbatim, TraceForge generates reasoning traces in the student model's own stylistic distribution conditioned on teacher answers, then applies supervised fine-tuning followed by an adversarial pass that penalizes traces the student couldn't plausibly have produced. The authors report meaningful gains on HumanEval+ and MBPP+, suggesting that trace-style match matters for distillation into coding agents. The method is simple enough to layer on top of existing SFT pipelines.
   Theme: (1) Core LLM advances (reasoning / fine-tuning)
   Relevance: Practical PEFT-adjacent recipe for distilling reasoning without the usual style-mismatch loss, with measured coding-benchmark wins.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29680

9. **Is Complex Agentic Memory Always Needed? Naive RAG Is Stronger Than We Thought**
   Authors: Zhengye Bao, Shengtao Zhang, Haokai Zhang, Shiqi Lou, Muning Wen
   Summary: The paper carefully re-benchmarks several recent "agentic memory" systems against a plain retrieval baseline over raw conversation history and finds the simple baseline often wins — once prompting, retrieval depth, and evaluation setup are made comparable. The authors attribute many reported gains to confounders (longer contexts, tuned prompts, favorable eval splits) rather than the memory architecture itself. The result is a sobering empirical check that reshapes the baseline bar for agentic-memory evaluation.
   Theme: (1) Core LLM advances (agentic-system evaluation)
   Relevance: A rigorous null-result paper that tightens what counts as a real win in agentic-memory evaluation — directly useful for anyone comparing agent designs.
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30755

10. **Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models (LM2LM)**
    Authors: Minhua Lin, Zijun Wang, Zewen Liu
    Summary: LM2LM uses reinforcement learning to train an LLM whose job is to propose improvements to LM development pipelines (data mixes, training recipes, evaluation tweaks), rewarded by a frozen "research world model" whose predictions are periodically grounded against real training runs. Across eight development environments, a 27B student trained this way outperforms a larger baseline at roughly a third lower data-generation cost. The result is one of the more concrete demonstrations of self-improving training loops at a non-trivial scale.
    Theme: (1) Core LLM advances (reasoning / self-improvement)
    Relevance: Novel RL recipe for recursive self-improvement with grounding against real executions; relevant to anyone studying agentic planners that modify their own training.
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23344
